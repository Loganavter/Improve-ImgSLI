# Development History

This document tracks the evolution of Improve-ImgSLI across releases and major refactors. It is migrated from the former "Development Story" section of README to keep the main page concise.

## 22 October 2024 — First prototype
The journey of Improve ImgSLI began on October 22, 2024, initially conceived with Claude 3.5 Sonnet's assistance to address a personal need for straightforward image comparison in my work. The early version provided basic side-by-side functionality. That very first script predates the repository history and is preserved as an artifact at [docs/origin/image-comparison-app.py](origin/image-comparison-app.py) — a single ~130-line PyQt6 widget with two image pickers, a split slider, a horizontal/vertical toggle, and PNG export.

## Late October 2024 — Magnifier and drag-and-drop
Later that month, another enhancement phase using Claude introduced a magnifier tool and drag-and-drop support, significantly improving usability.

## November 2024 — Magnifier refinements
By November, further refinements to the magnifier were implemented with Claude, including view freezing and merging capabilities. However, the codebase's expansion, coupled with Claude's 8k token context limit, made complete code regeneration impractical, necessitating manual integration of new features. Gemini was then employed to help manage these integrations, though not all AI-generated code was directly usable, leading to some features being postponed or temporarily disabled to maintain stability.

## December 2024 — Experiments and lessons
A brief period in early December saw an experiment with adaptive magnifier positioning tied to window resizing, which was ultimately set aside due to unsatisfactory results.

## January 2025 — Dynamic resizing
A turning point came in January 2025 when a user request for dynamic window resizing led to the exploration of DeepSeek. This AI, notable at the time for its Chain-of-Thought reasoning, successfully guided the implementation of this crucial feature, with Gemini then seamlessly incorporating it alongside other updates. See issue: https://github.com/Loganavter/Improve-ImgSLI/issues/1

## February 2025 — Feature growth and stability
February 2025 brought renewed focus on enhancing the application. Leveraging Claude Sonnet 3.7, features like dynamic image swapping, a multilingual dictionary, and further magnifier improvements were added, along with a helpful tooltip. Shortly thereafter, access to Grok 3—first via X, then its dedicated website—provided a significant boost. Grok 3's DeepThink model proved exceptionally effective at resolving persistent bugs, outperforming previous AIs, while its generous query limits and intelligent standard model streamlined development. Key contributions included optimized rendering, filename display and editing, and fixes for fullscreen mode.

## Late March 2025 — Cross-platform push
Late March 2025 was dedicated to cross-platform compatibility. Gemini and Grok, using their web search capabilities, assisted in drafting build and packaging scripts. While initial hopes for community maintenance of platform builds didn't materialize due to some skepticism about the project's AI-assisted origins, this solo effort, though time-intensive (taking considerable effort per platform), led to the discovery and resolution of several bugs, culminating in a successful cross-platform launch. Delays in Flathub publication until mid-April allowed for further bug fixes and the addition of a list cleanup feature.

## End of March 2025 — Timeline completion and architectural cleanup
By the end of March 2025, after several more days of messy but productive work with Claude and Codex, the long-running timeline overhaul was finally brought to a proper stopping point. The design was at last finished, and the overall structure became solid enough to prepare the feature for future editing capabilities rather than just passive viewing. At the same time, the contract between the viewing layer and the editing layer still felt insufficiently mature, so the final transition from a viewing-oriented timeline to a full editing workflow was left for a later stage.

This same stretch of work also turned into a broader cleanup pass across the application. Wherever possible, popup-like widgets were removed and rewritten as same-window integrated widgets drawn directly inside the main interface. Roughly 30% of the architecture was revisited again over the course of several more days. QSS styles were no longer concentrated in a single shared file and were instead split across the project according to responsibility. Windows started behaving much more predictably, most of the remaining core monoliths were dismantled, MVP was brought back to a cleaner and more correct form, and some parts of the code were optimized by extracting repeated logic into reusable helpers and services.

## April 2025 — Persistence and settings
In April 2025, attention shifted back to addressing critical bugs, with continued reliance on Gemini. A major achievement during this period was the implementation of window state persistence—a feature that proved incredibly challenging but vastly improved user experience. A settings tab was also introduced, centralizing language selection, output image quality, and filename length preferences. Following these updates, Windows and Flatpak builds were refreshed. The current plan involves implementing one final feature and verifying the Flatpak maintenance pipeline before updating Windows and AUR builds, after which the project will likely enter a period of stability.

## May 2025 — Caching and architecture split
May 2025. I discovered Cursor AI, which allowed me to quickly implement three key features in this update using agents like ChatGPT 4.1, Sonnet 3.5, and Gemini 2.5 PRO, all within the free limit of 150 uses. These features included a smarter caching service that improved smoothness by approximately 2-3 times compared to the previous version, the ability to select magnifier interpolation, and quick image preview using the keyboard. Additionally, the project's structure became much more multi-layered and less coupled between modules. This refactoring required two days of collaboration with Gemini to break down two monolithic scripts into numerous services with separate folders. The entire process took about three days.

## June 2025 — UI style refactor
June 2025. With Gemini, I refactored the code once again. The application now features a qfluentwidgets style. It initially took a long time, but then I gave the project fork code with this GUI, and it went much faster, it took about 3 days. Caching was also further improved. The icons.py file was completely removed as it was no longer necessary.

## July 2025 — Rendering pipeline overhaul
July 2025. The solid architecture from the June refactoring allowed for another rapid development burst. This update took about 4-5 days, again with Gemini's assistance. The launcher.sh script was turned into a proper command-line utility for managing the environment. The rendering pipeline was overhauled to use a dynamic canvas, which allows the magnifier to draw outside the image bounds and also improves performance on large images and magnifiers due to more efficient data packaging. The magnifier also now correctly handles horizontal splitting. UI interaction was improved with long-press actions and scrollable selection dropdowns. Internally, all print statements were replaced with a standard logging system. The persistent bug with window geometry, however, remains. After over 20 failed attempts to fix it, it's clear the root cause is a race condition in how state signals are handled. This confirms the need to move away from the current semblance of an architecture to a full MVC/MVVM pattern, which will be the primary focus going forward.

## August 2025 — Full custom UI and MVP
August 2025. This update was born out of necessity. The qfluentwidgets library, while initially useful, became a major roadblock due to its extreme rigidity in customization. Discovering that simple layout logic was hardcoded in its C++ source was the final straw. This prompted a full rewrite of the user interface with 100% custom components. In parallel, the long-standing "god script" issue was tackled by migrating the entire application to a proper Model-View-Presenter (MVP) architecture. This new, solid foundation allowed for the rapid implementation of several new features: an image rating system, selectable light/dark themes, a display cache for performance with large files, and completely redesigned settings and help dialogs. The entire transformation was completed in about a week, exclusively with Gemini's help.

## Early October 2025 — Shared toolkit and UX polish
Early October 2025. With renewed access to Cursor AI, a significant unification of the codebase with another of my projects, Tkonverter, was carried out. The result is a shared library, shared_toolkit, which simplifies transferring functionality between projects. Following a user request, a feature for pasting images from the clipboard (Ctrl+V) was added. The user experience was substantially redesigned, introducing new customization options for the main comparison line and the magnifier's divider (controlling visibility, color, and thickness). System notifications on Linux were also fixed. In addition to Cursor, this update was developed with the active use of Gemini and Claude models, as well as new AI aggregator platforms, which helped accelerate the process. See issue: https://github.com/Loganavter/Improve-ImgSLI/issues/20
Further enhancements to the magnifier (precision controls and subpixel rendering for smoother comparisons between different resolutions), plus the addition of pixel/structural difference views and analysis tools (channels, edges, metrics). This batch of work took approximately 3–5 hours.

## December 2025 – January 2026 — Plugin Architecture and Video Export
This update represents a massive architectural overhaul that took nearly a week of intensive work. After experimenting with several patterns, including Redux-like state management, the application was migrated to a more flexible and scalable plugin-based architecture.

The flagship feature of this release is the introduction of a video editor and export functionality, allowing users to record their comparison sessions, perform basic trimming, and export the result to various video formats. The user experience of the main toolbar was also redesigned for clarity and better grouping of functions.

Several quality-of-life improvements were added, such as automatic cropping of black borders on image load, magnifier guide lines (or "lasers") to visually connect the magnifier to its capture point, and support for the JPEG XL format. This release was a significant effort to stabilize the new architecture and deliver a cohesive set of powerful new features. Development was primarily done using Cursor AI with models from Claude and ChatGPT, as well as separately using Gemini 3.5 Pro Preview.

## February–March 2026 — Architectural Overhaul and Hybrid Rendering
This period saw a two-part, substantial update focused on deep architectural refactoring and performance. The first part involved a complete overhaul of the canvas architecture. This long-overdue refactor resulted in a highly optimized system, with all operating modes, comparison types, and edge cases fully debugged and stabilized. The second part introduced the most significant new feature: hybrid CPU-GPU rendering. This effort also involved another deep dive into the architecture, further breaking down services into sub-services and plugins.

The hybrid rendering system was developed with the help of Claude Code and Opus 4.6 (despite their restrictive quotas). Together, we designed a transition framework for scaling the entire rendering subsystem, which included writing approximately 80% of the new shaders. The process took several days, as the legacy pipeline was heavily optimized for the CPU, leading to unexpected bugs when integrating the new GPU-based components. For more applied tasks, ChatGPT Codex and ChatGPT 5.4 were used; they proved to be much faster and highly capable, assisting with tasks other than the core rendering system.

In another major step forward, all pop-ups and separate windows—which previously mimicked being part of the main window—were rewritten as native Qt widgets with Claude's assistance. The only exception was the image list pop-up, which was meticulously redesigned to be pixel-perfect for the first time since its initial implementation in early 2025. This change brings the application significantly closer to running natively on Wayland without the X11 compatibility layer, though some nuances still need to be addressed. The conversion of most widgets was surprisingly fast, taking about an hour, but the image list required experimenting with nearly 10 different architectural approaches to find the optimal layout and rendering method.

The video editor also received a significant overhaul:
*   The preview renderer can now fill empty areas with any custom color.
*   The timeline was rewritten from the ground up to be more robust and intuitive. Thumbnails now preserve their aspect ratio and are rendered in high quality.
*   Timeline markers are now dynamically spaced based on thumbnail dimensions rather than being tied to a hardcoded 16:9 aspect ratio and video time.
*   Internally, the editor moved from a snapshot-oriented approach to a keyframe-based model, laying the groundwork for proper editing.
This work took around 2.5 hours.

A key new feature is the addition of canvas zooming and panning. This was implemented in just about 3 hours but involved complex mathematics developed with Opus and ChatGPT.

The program was also prepared for the eventual introduction of workspaces, although no new tabs are expected in the immediate updates. The longer-term plan is to extend this foundation toward video comparison, but first I still need to finish several pending improvements in the core image-comparison workflow: the video editor itself is not fully finished yet, the filename/text overlay system still needs more work, and a color picker is also planned.

In total, this series of updates took over a week of late-night coding sessions. While I'm not convinced that a CLI-first workflow is definitively superior to IDEs, the higher quotas were a significant advantage over tools like Cursor. However, Cursor excels in facilitating learning, as it allows you to visually absorb new code patterns during development. I believe that developing the UI toolkit without an IDE like it would be unrealistic. A broader distribution of everything accumulated since November 2025 is planned slightly later as version 8.0.0.

## Early April 2026 — Compatibility patching and release pipeline hardening
Early April 2026 turned into a cleanup phase after the huge February-March refactor. Most of the work here was not about adding flashy new features, but about finally chasing down the annoying edge cases that only became visible once zooming, panning, hybrid rendering, and the new backend split started interacting with each other. With Codex and ChatGPT 5.4, I spent a lot of time fixing split-line behavior near the image borders, correcting magnifier geometry and clamping, and reducing high-DPI mismatches where the visible render area and the actual interaction area did not quite agree.

Another major topic was startup behavior and backend compatibility. The program moved further toward the Quick canvas path, because the old OpenGL widget path kept exposing more platform-specific quirks than I wanted to keep fighting forever. The prewarm and reveal logic also had to be reconsidered. Right now that old startup behavior still works best on GNOME Wayland; on KDE Wayland and Windows it showed various compositor and window-lifecycle artifacts. Those fixes are not finished yet and will need a separate round of testing later, so for now this part should be considered only partially solved.

The video editor also got a small but important safety fix: trimming without an actual selection no longer deletes the whole clip. At the same time, version 8.2.0 packaging was cleaned up across Flatpak, AUR, and Windows templates, and a first proper Windows GitHub Actions pipeline was added. It now builds the PyInstaller bundle and the Inno Setup installer automatically, and before doing so it checks that release metadata is synchronized across the packaging files. This work was much less glamorous than the renderer rewrite, but it was necessary if the project was going to start behaving like a real maintained application rather than just a constantly changing codebase.

## Early May 2026 — Canvas feature contract and multi-magnifier work
Early May 2026 became another architectural pass over the canvas renderer. Over roughly a week, using Codex with ChatGPT 5.4 and 5.5, the rendering model was reviewed again. Despite the previous refactors, the canvas still turned out to be much more tightly coupled than it should have been: adding or changing one visual feature still required touching scene construction, reducers, rendering code, settings, editor tracks, and other unrelated places. 

The main result of this work was the beginning of a dedicated canvas feature contract, inspired by the way larger graphics applications such as GIMP, Krita, Photoshop, and similar editors keep many tools manageable without turning the renderer into anarchy. In addition to the architectural shift, the rendering process was moved to the GPU; however, this remains a work in progress, with further fine-tuning needed to achieve an ideal visual result. The contract is not perfect yet, but it moves scene building, applying, hit testing, settings, and keyframe/editor metadata toward a single feature-owned registration model. The goal is that by version 10 or 11 the remaining feature logic will be consolidated enough that adding or removing a canvas feature no longer requires chasing hardcoded references across the project.

The magnifier also received a major expansion during this period. It is now possible to create more than one magnifier, color them independently, and use additional highlight logic to make interactions with multiple magnifiers more intuitive. This includes helper highlighting for hidden or occluded capture areas and better behavior around deletion, hiding, focus, export, zooming, guide lines, and comparison modes. The multi-magnifier system is still expected to evolve further in later updates, but this release establishes the first usable foundation.

Another packaging-related cleanup was the removal of the notification path that depended directly on DBus. Flatpak did not handle that approach well enough, so the notification behavior was simplified to avoid that problematic dependency.

At the same time, the UI toolkit extraction reached a much more practical stage. The reusable widget layer is now close to being a real plug-in-style library instead of an internal folder of the application, with the public API, styling bridge, and widget contract pushed far enough that most future UI work can build on it without reopening the whole application tree every time.

## Mid-May 2026 — Decoupling the Core and Maturing the Feature Contract

Building on the work from early May, the middle of the month was dedicated to a much deeper and more aggressive refactoring of the feature contract system, again with the help of Claude Opus and Codex. The core problem was that despite the new contract system, features were still too tightly coupled with the rest of the application. References to specific features, like the "magnifier," were scattered across core modules and event handlers, creating a tangled web that was essentially well-disguised spaghetti code.

This half-week effort completely reworked the system to rely strictly on contracts and registrations. The result is a far more maintainable and robust architecture. The change immediately resolved several nagging problems, such as correctly managing scaling and supporting five different rendering modes for the main scene. The true measure of success is the new level of modularity: previously, deleting a feature's folder would cause a fatal crash on startup. Now, the application handles such a scenario gracefully, reporting a localized error like "magnifier.size is not defined" or simply failing to respond to a specific button press, rather than bringing down the entire program. While I'm not completely satisfied with the result, this overhaul has finally eliminated the worst of the architectural entanglement, making the system far less shameful to work with.

Still, some work remains on optimizing a couple of canvas features. The tab system has been tested and works normally, but a new tab will not be implemented until June or July at the earliest. The UI toolkit has finally been extracted into a package form, though not completely standalone for a couple of technical reasons; it has also been renamed to "sli", as it no longer shares much with "fluent design". The documentation now describes the established design language.

## Late May 2026 — Test suite, full zoom, and release readiness

The final stretch of May turned into a consolidation phase, working with Claude and Codex. The biggest structural addition was a proper automated test suite — roughly 480 test cases covering the architectural contracts (feature manifests, capability aliases, GL pass stacking, tab and plugin isolation), behavioral runtime contracts (reducer purity, viewport-change emission, gesture resolution, EventBus depth guarding), settings persistence, and the video/keyframe pipeline. The tests are parameterized so that new features are automatically pulled under the existing rules without rewriting them, and `launcher.sh` gained a `test` command. With this in place the architecture finally feels genuinely adequate rather than just "less bad": deleting a feature degrades gracefully, the contracts are enforced mechanically, and the previously formalized viewport-change contract is now backed by regression tests. The magnifier also received fixes for various rendering and state-management issues, and the render pipeline now handles reads and writes reliably, with state changes propagating to the UI and all scene modes behaving as expected.

The zoom system was reworked into a full implementation. Where the canvas previously only handled magnification at 100% and above, it now supports a continuous range from roughly 10% up to very high magnification, with proper panning across the zoomed viewport.

A very large number of bugs were fixed in this period, including several rendering and drawing issues. Substantial work also went into the keyframe-read side of the render pipeline; it is functional, but I'm still not satisfied with its overall pattern, so it will likely be revisited. The keyframe-write pipeline and the interactive scene, by contrast, received many concrete bug fixes and are in much better shape.

At this point the application is fully stable and ready to ship as version 9.

## Early June 2026 — UI toolkit finally lives on its own

In early June the shared UI toolkit was at last fully extracted from both Improve-ImgSLI and tkonverter into a standalone repository. It is not on PyPI yet, but both applications now consume it as an external package rather than reaching into an internal folder — which on its own already feels like a much healthier setup than what came before.

Once the library was out on its own, it went through an extremely, extremely, extremely, extremely, extremely... large refactoring pass. The result is a toolkit that has dramatically fewer bugs and rendering glitches, is noticeably simpler to configure, and at the same time exposes much more customization than the embedded version ever did. Variants, themes, flyout timings, label specs, and the widget contract are all reachable from the outside now, without forcing consumers to monkey-patch internals.

For the immediate future the plan is to keep pushing on the library itself — more polish, more API smoothing, and probably preparing it for a real PyPI release — before turning attention back to Improve-ImgSLI proper. The application is stable enough at version 9 that it can wait while the foundation underneath it gets the attention it deserves.

## June 2026 — QRhi migration, custom windowing, and workspace expansion

June 2026 became one of the most exhausting development periods in the project so far. What was supposed to be a continuation of the UI toolkit work quickly turned into a chain of deep renderer, windowing, and workspace changes, with several painful detours along the way.

The shared toolkit went through another very large refactoring pass and was reshaped toward a configuration style closer to Blender: less hardcoded widget behavior, more explicit external configuration, and a cleaner contract for styling and interaction. Ripple support was added, along with a number of smaller interaction and customization improvements. This took far longer than expected, but the toolkit is now much more suitable as a real foundation rather than just a pile of reused widgets.

A major renderer shift also happened after I learned that OpenGL is increasingly becoming a legacy path on modern platforms and may eventually depend more on compatibility and translation layers. Because of that, I urgently started moving the GLSL-based rendering path toward Qt’s QRhi abstraction in order to open the door to Vulkan and Metal backends. In practice, the transition was not as catastrophic as it first sounded: GLSL and QRhi are not identical, but the existing shader logic could be adapted without completely redesigning the rendering model.

At the same time, both the toolkit and the main application moved from PyQt to PySide. The main motivation was long-term maintenance: PySide tends to track Qt updates more closely and should hopefully contain fewer unpleasant surprises. The migration itself was relatively quick because most of the work was mechanical import and namespace replacement, but it still touched a large portion of the codebase.

QRhi then exposed a new platform-specific problem. Under Vulkan on Mutter/GNOME, native window decorations behaved badly enough that keeping the system frame no longer felt reliable. As a result, the application started moving in the same general direction as Telegram Desktop: drawing and managing its own window frame. This pushed the UI even further away from a conventional Qt desktop application and closer to a fully custom shell. At this point the project increasingly feels like a mixture of Telegram Desktop, Firefox, PyQt-Fluent, Blender, and Photoshop. In future updates, the system decoration will probably be removed entirely, with more functionality moving into the top header area, closer to applications like Steam or VS Code.

The most frustrating detour of the month was the attempt to solve the first-frame flicker problem by porting parts of the application to C++ and Rust. I went into this with the naive assumption that, since PyQt and PySide are ultimately bindings over C++ Qt, a language port would mostly be a matter of translation. Recent examples of LLM-assisted language migrations made this seem more realistic than it actually was. In practice, the experiment showed the opposite: LLMs can help with a port only when there is already a strong technical foundation, a clear architecture map, a build plan, and a migration strategy. Without that preparation, the process becomes expensive, noisy, and mentally exhausting. After several days, roughly $20 of agent/API work, and a lot of frustration, the entire port was thrown away. The useful lesson was not the port itself, but the realization that this kind of migration has to be prepared like a real engineering project, not handed to an autonomous agent with the hope that it will simply figure everything out. Ironically, after abandoning the port, the original flickering issue was solved in Python with a much smaller set of targeted fixes.

The tab system was also reactivated for the first time in a long while and restyled toward a modern Firefox-like tab bar. This finally made the workspace foundation feel alive again. The second workspace, focused on multi-comparison, was significantly harder to design and implement. It went through many iterations, from a more monolithic prototype to a structure closer to the existing image comparison system. The final version is usable, although a few architectural compromises remain and will need to be cleaned up in future updates.

Another visible addition was the first real context menu in the application. Surprisingly, this part did not cause much trouble because the necessary contract-based foundation had already been laid earlier. It became one of the first places where the previous architectural work paid off directly instead of merely preventing the application from collapsing under its own complexity.

Overall, this update took a disproportionate amount of time, money, and mental energy compared with its apparent feature list. Still, the application gained a more future-proof rendering direction, a PySide foundation, custom windowing groundwork, a revived tab system, a new multi-comparison workspace, better UX, and several important lessons about the limits of vibe coding at larger scale. The project is becoming less like a simple image comparison tool and more like a small creative desktop environment built around a custom Qt-based design system.

## Late June – Early July 2026 — Image Compare becomes a workspace and the platform transition begins

The end of June and the beginning of July became the final stage of one of the largest architectural transitions in the project's history. The image comparison system, which for a long time was deeply embedded into the application's core, was finally extracted into a standalone tab-based workspace instead of being treated as a fundamental part of the application itself.

This refactor was extremely painful. The previous failed C++/Rust migration attempt was not the only difficult architectural challenge anymore — now the goal was to separate years of accumulated assumptions and move them behind a clean tab contract. The breakthrough came when I forced Claude to stop attempting direct implementation and instead create a very large step-by-step migration plan, consisting of around 39 individual stages, with progress reports written into a separate document. This workflow proved dramatically more effective than the previous autonomous migration attempt. It still required a lot of manual involvement, debugging, and architectural decisions, but the overall migration was completed successfully in approximately four days.

After the main extraction, the next phase became cleanup and stabilization. Some internal connections had broken, the QRhi renderer still had unreliable startup behavior in certain situations, and several contracts needed additional work to prevent subtle UX issues. The result is not perfect yet, but it is much closer to the intended vision.

The application now opens through a session picker, where the user can select between the currently available workspaces. Switching between tabs is handled as a proper application-level transition instead of a collection of hidden state changes. This finally gives the application a more professional desktop-software feeling rather than a single-purpose tool with additional modes attached.

However, the multi-compare workspace still represents a significant remaining technical debt. It will likely be the main feature debt to resolve before the version 10 release. The current work on image comparison itself is still mostly about cleaning up the consequences of two major refactors that happened almost simultaneously:

* the migration from OpenGL to QRhi;
* the removal of deep image-comparison dependencies from the core in favor of a tab contract architecture.

Both changes were necessary, but together they created a large amount of cleanup work that is still being gradually resolved.

The UI toolkit also received another important round of development. Interestingly, the application LLM itself became an informal beta tester for the toolkit. Whenever the model attempted to directly modify the toolkit source code despite the documentation being available, the work was interrupted and the reason was investigated: what part of the API was unclear, insufficient, or inconvenient? That feedback was then used by a separate toolkit-focused agent together with the documentation to improve the actual design.

As a result, button regions were redesigned once again, making the API significantly more universal and flexible. Another major unification effort was also completed: widgets can now choose whether they should render as an in-app component or as a popup-style element, reducing the previous separation between these two concepts.

The tab bar itself was also migrated away from Qt's built-in QTabBar. Instead of spending time reverse-engineering and adapting its internal behavior, it was replaced with a custom widget built using the project's own toolkit. The reasoning was simple: a predictable custom component is often more valuable than a powerful but opaque framework component that requires constant workarounds.

By the end of July, the goal is to finish the remaining architectural cleanup and finally transform Improve-ImgSLI from an application into a platform. Version 10 is expected to represent this transition point: not just another feature update, but the moment when the underlying architecture becomes capable of hosting multiple independent creative workflows.

Another major planned change is licensing. The project will temporarily move from MIT to GPL-3.0 while I evaluate possible future monetization models. There are currently no meaningful external contributors, and the project is still primarily developed because I personally enjoy working on it. However, I want to avoid the common open-source situation where projects built under permissive licenses eventually struggle with sustainability while others freely build commercial products around them without contributing back.

This does not necessarily mean the codebase will remain GPL forever. Personally, I find some aspects of restrictive open-source licensing philosophically uncomfortable: the idea of "the source is visible, but you cannot really use it freely" has always felt somewhat unintuitive to me. From my perspective, software distribution has two clear extremes: either you openly share the code, or you keep it proprietary. The current GPL transition is therefore more of a temporary strategic decision while the project's future direction and possible sustainability model are being figured out.

## Mid-July 2026 — Tab encapsulation, GEGL-lite tiling, Cursor again, and a near-finished product

The middle of July began as a consolidation of the tab-contract work started in late June, and this time the encapsulation genuinely holds together rather than merely looking clean on paper. Tabs are now close to properly isolated from one another, with dependencies flowing through the contract instead of leaking around it.

For the image comparison tab, a tiling system was first added to the rendering pipeline, which started lifting the long-standing hard limit on images larger than 16k pixels. Early on it was not fully polished — there were still noticeable freezes at a couple of stages — and the equivalent mechanism had not yet been built for the multi-compare workspace, so that tab still carried the old restriction for a while.

Application performance also improved significantly in this period. It turned out that a large part of the sluggishness was not coming from the application's own code at all, but from the UI toolkit itself, which was polling far too many widgets far too often. Once that was identified and addressed, the improvement was immediately noticeable across the interface.

Beyond that, a broad scope of interface bugs and other smaller rough edges were cleaned up. The documentation went through a real revision pass: it is now fully up to date, rendering-related docs live in their own dedicated folder, and the overall map of the project is easier to follow — fewer thousand-line dumps and less irrelevant noise. A number of flat, unstructured patterns scattered across different parts of the codebase were also reorganized into something more coherent. A `TODO.md` was introduced, giving the project a public list of what is planned and considered committed for upcoming releases, rather than leaving that roadmap implicit.

I also decided to try Cursor again, and I was pleasantly surprised by both the speed and the quality of the work — especially compared with Claude Code. Cursor’s limits feel a bit tighter on paper, but the day-to-day experience is still more convenient than living under Claude Code’s five-hour and weekly quota walls. (Claude Code can reportedly be stretched to something like 10×+ the nominal subscription value if you work the quotas hard — at least according to forums and various write-ups — but that still doesn’t make the pacing nicer.) Having Cursor available again mattered: literally over about two days I was able to finish a GEGL-lite analogue for full-resolution pixel storage and bring both workspace tabs onto it.

That tiling/spill path (`TiledPixelStore`, strip write into memmap, GPU residency by tile) finally takes the old “hard ceiling around 16k” out of the product story for load and live work. Decode still materializes a full frame once — so pathological sizes remain a RAM concern — but spill no longer keeps a second full `HxWx4` copy, both Image Compare and Multi Compare share the same storage model, and still-image export above the tested 16k edge warns instead of silently clamping the composition plane.

In the same stretch the shell moved further toward an IDE-style custom chrome, and a Find Action palette landed — inspired by GIMP’s action search, but with this app’s own grouping, pulse-into-view behavior, and settings/export wiring. The in-app help system was fully reworked into a hierarchical hub/tree of scenario pages (host + tab contributions), and another large pile of bugs and UX rough edges got cleaned up along the way.

Overall the application feels about **100% ready** as a product for the current scope. Looking further ahead, the next workspace people will likely invent their way into is a dedicated video-comparison tab — not a near-term commitment, but the natural next mode once the existing tab foundation and comparison habits have been lived with for a while.

Finally, the long-awaited custom project container format (`.imgsli`) has been added, and the surrounding infrastructure is mostly implemented (though not 100% finalized yet). I burnt through my Cursor quota in just 2-3 days, but in that short time, I managed to invent the format and fix an astronomical amount of issues. It proved to be an incredibly effective tool for the final push, sustaining 10-16 hour work sessions. Cursor is impressive due to its highly advanced tooling — it could independently debug about 40% of the things that Claude CLI or Codex couldn't. However, its main flaw is that it takes quite a while to realize when it should switch to collaborative debugging with the user. 

To finish the release, I decided to try Kimi (buying a $20 subscription on sale), and it was frankly the worst investment of my life. Their limits are worse than Codex (which is hard to achieve), it works slower than both Codex and Sonnet 5, and it falls miles behind Cursor. I used it briefly, decided not to grind out the limit just for the sake of it, and moved on to test Gemini. Gemini pleasantly surprised me — while it might be slightly on the periphery of the frontier since the last release, their IDE works very fast. It's slightly worse than Cursor, but not critically so. The limits are good, and the models seem quite capable in their reasoning.

## Early August 2026 — A rocky v10 release, a rewritten toolkit, and the painful move to LODs

Version 10 shipped, and it did not go smoothly. The Flatpak submission got kicked back by the Flathub reviewers — I was missing proper license documentation for several bundled third-party libraries, and they were not shy about it. Separately, my AUR co-maintainer hadn't synced against a PKGBUILD change in my own repo in time, and the published package ended up with `sli-ui-toolkit` broken as a dependency. Between the two, v10 landed as a bit of a flop on both Linux channels. The only build that came out clean was the Windows binary.

Since then it's been mostly toolkit and rendering work, with Claude Sonnet 5 doing the bulk of the actual implementation across this whole stretch. `sli-ui-toolkit` went through another serious reorganization — I've lost count of how many times now — but this time the documentation is finally in real shape instead of thrown together, `mypy` is wired in, and a lot of the primitive and composite widgets got meaningfully improved. The UI input should just feel newer across the board.

The bigger story was rendering. The old tile system turned out to be genuinely buggy under the hood — it would work, then quietly stop working, with no obvious pattern — and the fix was moving to a proper mip-pyramid LOD stack instead of flat tiling. This was a genuinely painful stretch, something like a week or more of total effort just to get everything working reliably on top of it. A few new devtools came out of this period too, like a UI layout dumper, though in practice it hasn't turned out to be all that useful yet.

I also spent time with Qwen 3.8 Max talking through a redesign of the Image Compare tab's chrome. The bottom HUDs are now an Apple-glass-style flyout instead of a permanent full-width row that used to eat into the canvas's vertical space. Getting there was very painful — but the debugging along the way taught me a lot about how QRhi and my own tiling system actually behave under pressure. The zoom HUD got the same treatment afterward. Following the same AI-assisted design pass, the magnifier HUD moved into its own flyout too, this time without the glass effect — it now reads like the interface stretches downward, as if you pulled a sheet down, rather than a floating glass panel. Same space-saving idea, and more intuitive to use. Getting there meant reinventing the flyout manager and then a long stretch of debugging afterward, but it holds up now.

The color picker dialog is also self-built now instead of falling back to the OS-native one, though it's still fairly bare-bones — more work planned there. The scrollable toolbar buttons got a UX pass too, which turned out to be another genuinely painful one — it meant fighting with the toolkit's underline system — but the button is noticeably more capable and more informative now. Beyond that, a long tail of small UX fixes landed: context menus were switched over to the library-provided one everywhere instead of ad hoc ones, and click handling now waits for a ripple effect to finish before opening its target window, instead of letting the ripple freeze halfway through.

Looking ahead, the plan is to push the Apple-glass-style treatment further across the app to keep up with current design trends, which will mean going back into the toolkit again — though that pass shouldn't be as heavy as this one. On the product side, the main idea floating around is a dedicated gallery tab with AI-backed functionality, and a general push toward more AI presence in the app overall — though beyond the gallery tab, what exactly that looks like is still an open question.

## Mid-August 2026 — A GEGL-shaped goal, a LOD marathon, and the day the repository deleted itself

The middle of August was swallowed by the move to a proper tiled system — an effort that ultimately stretched across something like six weeks, from the first LOD work in late July through to the tile-engine consolidation in mid-September. My target was something GEGL-like: a single full-resolution pixel store with a clean render engine sitting on top of it. The agent, however, kept steering the design toward a large set of separate tile levels, and when I pushed back, several other models I consulted ended up agreeing with it. I did not share their confidence, but I went along with it anyway. What followed was an unreasonable amount of time — the better part of a week or more — spent simply making the transitions between levels behave. That work then uncovered a deeper problem: the codebase had accumulated something on the order of twenty intermediate caches, one for nearly every situation. Cleaning that out — and then discovering a second and a third layer of the same problem — eventually pushed us to rebuild the general render engine properly, along established best practices, purely out of exhaustion with the ad-hoc structure.

The final design is reliable, but it took an enormous number of refactors and more than one round of architectural second-guessing. Across this stretch the day-to-day implementation was carried mostly by DeepSeek V4 Flash and Muse Spark Contributor 1.2/1.3, on top of earlier Claude Sonnet 5 work; the testing was mine. This is also when I switched to opencode (the Go plan) as my main environment. I have been paying as I go — two $5 subscriptions across this stretch, then the latest one at $10 — and the unexpected upside is that the quota is genuinely hard to exhaust, so there is almost always work in flight.

The immediate context of the disaster was the launcher rewrite: I had replaced the old multi-hundred-line bash launcher — Linux/bash-4-bound, with no Windows entry point at all — by a stdlib-only Python core behind thin `launcher.sh` and `launcher.bat` wrappers, together with a fresh test suite. It was one of those new tests that deleted the repository.

Then came the catastrophe. An agent wrote a test that accidentally deleted the repository directory — the entire working tree, `.git` included. Worse, the same test fired again during the first hasty recovery attempt, wiping the partially restored tree, and a third time during the necromancy run itself. The final reconstruction took about a day and was done by replaying the AI-session logs: the opencode database, which held every session from 09.08 to 16.08, plus the earlier Claude session transcripts, all stacked onto a clean snapshot taken at the Claude-to-opencode switchover around 10.08. Because every write, edit and read had been recorded with full file contents and timestamps, those logs effectively acted as a filesystem snapshot server; the replay brought back roughly a week of work with only about 3.5% of edits missing, and what was missing was content that had never existed in the logs at all. I then made sure it could not happen again: a daemon now commits and pushes everything to GitHub on a timer (though it did not prevent the next incident), and I pulled the agent-work documentation into a separate private repository — partly to keep those logs off the public project, and partly because working on this codebase without them is close to impossible; reconstructing even a single working shader pass from the source alone would be a real fight.

## Late August 2026 — Toolkit, again, and again

In parallel the toolkit went through several more overhauls in quick succession — over the course of a week or so. Among them: wiring `mypy` across the whole package, rebuilding keyboard navigation around an explicit focus graph instead of scattered `setFocus` calls, and reworking the inspector's layout and widget-description system. This was once again largely DeepSeek and Muse writing, with me on testing.

## Early September 2026 — Orchestration, Redux, and tab isolation

Around the start of September I decided to take orchestration seriously and automate it properly. After a couple of passes over roughly ten days it came together into a workable setup, though I am still fixing its bugs. At some point it began opening pull requests against the public `main`; after a short incident with commits I reworked the system once more, and now it runs under a separate account with a deliberately reduced permission set.

Redux I fixed roughly five times over. As it turned out, the tests were only exercising the dispatch half of the state layer, and Redux was effectively absent almost everywhere in practice. It is standardised now, but the process was tedious.

Tab isolation was a long-running sore point of the same kind: for a while it was isolated only in name, and turning it into a real boundary took a great deal of refactoring and repeated architecture passes — the agent reliably proposing everything except the correct pattern before we finally got there. The Multi-Compare pipeline went through the same treatment. Alongside this, the theme and colour system was restructured into something that now behaves predictably, the help window was reworked into a readable hub, and there was the "lost three days" incident, where a large batch of branches had not been merged back into `main` and had to be folded in bulk over the course of a single evening.

## Mid-September 2026 — Image Compare redesign, interface scaling, and the tile engine

The Image Compare tab got a long-overdue redesign aimed at reclaiming space for the canvas. The bottom info HUD became an Apple-glass-style element instead of a full-width backdrop, and the controls moved into flyout panels that slide in and out. It came out far from the first attempt — I had to rework parts of the toolkit to get there — but it was worth it. Window resize speed was improved substantially, and window-interaction handling was reworked more broadly.

All of this fed into the final consolidation of the general render engine into a proper tile engine — four migration epics landed in a three-day push in mid-September — which closed out a very long arc. The AI gallery also finally moved off the ground: its main screen was roughly assembled from Qwen sketches, though it is still an alpha. What remains is deciding what kind of agent tool can generate layouts on top of my toolkit without making a mess of it. Still ahead: the video editor and interaction management need more work, the gallery is early, and the help system wants another refactor. Overall, the application is in a relatively working state.

## Late September 2026 — The engine grows up, the timeline is rewritten, and the gallery overhaul begins

Sometime around the middle of September I finally sat down and looked hard at the engine, and it turned out to be a bit of a mess. Only part of the systematic work actually lived inside it; the two comparison tabs each implemented the same interchangeable mechanisms in radically different ways, and trying to build the gallery tab on top of that was its own special kind of hell. So I moved everything that belonged there into the engine and cleaned up the seams. It behaves far better now — though the real payoff will not be obvious until the next tab gets built on it. Around the same time I also added demand-driven LOD loading: levels are now built and pulled on demand instead of all at once.

That work then surfaced the next sore spot: the timeline tab had been written badly enough that it had to be refactored almost down to nothing.

Somewhere in there a whole pile of my orchestration problems came to the surface — and rather late. Beyond the hours that went into minute-long actions, the git machinery itself — backups, merges, and everything around them — had, until that point, been set up very crudely, and the "technical work" it demanded could run to several hours before any actual work could begin. The agent then went and read the papers and the self-reporting patterns people use per session, and reworked the whole setup itself. Work is no longer lost when a session restarts, and fixing a bug is dramatically faster.

All of that was compounded by the model I was working with at the time — Muse Spark v1.3 Contributor — whose RLHF was poor enough that it would overthink everything and drag me down with it. (To be fair, the whole setup was dim back then.) Four hours into a debugging session it was entirely normal for it to propose logging processor ticks and commands, or filing a report against Qt or Mutter — an issue, not a PR, and I kept shutting that down. I was on the edge of burnout, like sitting twenty hours behind a machine-gun sight scanning for drones. And I was always right: the actual fix was almost always a couple of lines of code.

Once the comparison tabs finally felt, at last, about 200% done, I turned to a full overhaul of the gallery tab. It had been partially assembled roughly half a month earlier, and crudely. Claude had produced three SVG mockups to start from, and I handed the implementation to Muse Spark Contributor. It did not go well — I ended up doing a great deal of it myself. About halfway through I stumbled onto the fact that it could actually recognise images, which sped things up a little, though not much. After roughly a week of grinding we had the tab put together. The mockup, as it turned out, was pretty and bad at UX, so I rebuilt parts of it on the fly and swapped the flyouts for right-click context menus.

## Early October 2026 — The gallery, and an AI that ends up without AI

By around 7–10 October the gallery at last looked visually finished, and I assumed only the image-analysis AI was left. It was not. The results looked off from the start: the model had decided, for no discernible reason, to reinvent the arithmetic — hand-writing every analyser instead of borrowing an existing open-source one. I had meant to hand the whole judgement over to a VLM, so I did, and that was nonsense too. I had by then swapped Muse Spark Contributor out for DeepSeek V4.1 (Muse Spark was dimmer and nearly twice as slow). With a far more refined model and the orchestration fixes finally in place, the process sped up, the technical work fell away, and a sense of progress returned in place of a stagnant swamp — I could even watch a movie in between. I sent it to survey the literature on how accurate VLMs actually are at spotting image defects. The answer was deflating: 54% — barely more often right than a randomiser, which is free. So I sent it looking for alternatives, for algorithms or purpose-built models with measured results. It found some, downloaded them, measured them itself, and we replaced the model's judgement with standard algorithms. There were a few technical and UX problems, but nothing dramatic.

So as it stands, the "AI gallery" I set out to build gets by entirely without AI. Both funny and sad. A little more time and this long-overdue update is finished. Really.

## Mid-October 2026 — Fragile plumbing, a correction on the VLM, and handing the keyboard to the agents

That "little more time" turned into another week of plumbing, and the plumbing is where the week went. A lot of the internals turned out to be, bluntly, shit. Translations are the clean example: they break on the slightest sloppy code — one hard-coded string, or a key duplicated into the wrong i18n root, and the whole thing falls over. Theming had the same disease from the other end: a widget that resolves a colour or an icon once, at build time, freezes that value forever, so a theme flip leaves half the interface behind. The agent worked out a systemic, pattern-level fix for that whole class — resolve the derived value lazily at paint time, give the refresh an owned order, and put a CI gate in front of it so the next offender fails instead of shipping. It holds. It also cost a serious amount of quota, and a serious amount of my time.

The gallery's AI went through several rebuilds of its design along the way — partly because the agent and I were carrying different pictures of what the thing was supposed to be, and each pass exposed the disagreement. We only converged after a few reworkings.

And I owe the VLM an apology. The 54% I quoted last time was wrong; the real figure is around 65%, and for judging image defects by eye that is genuinely good — expert level, not the coin-flip I made it out to be. So the AI backend was rebuilt properly and handed both jobs, analysis and ranking, with the algorithmic path always there to pick instead. The AI-gallery design got polished to match while we were at it.

By the 17th–18th I had come round to building an MCP surface into the app itself, so that as much of the testing as possible could be pushed onto agents. My interface-control system is a little non-standard — it is built around the keyboard — and verifying it by hand is expensive in exactly the way agents are not.

The help system also got a full refactor. I first tried to carry it through Muse, since the volume is enormous — but it was extremely dumb and slop-prone, and I burned a lot of time and effort on it for nothing. Reconnected DeepSeek and we were finished in a couple of hours.

None of it came cheap, either — and not in the way I had budgeted. The refactors I had not planned, the theming pattern pass, the i18n fragility, the help rewrite, ate through the paid quota far faster than I expected, so for a stretch I was living on the free newcomers. Space Bunny (MiniMax M3.1 Flash underneath) is genuinely fun to work with, but the limits are crap; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM-5.3 Flash are the same story. LongCat 2.5 Preview is the interesting one — but only if you start the session on something smarter and let LongCat continue it; started cold, it is a bit dim, for reasons I never did pin down.

Right now another systemic refactor of the app's backend is underway, together with the keyboard interface control, and after that the update ships. Far from everything I have planned is in — but it is usable without feeling half-finished; what is left is unrealised potential rather than loose ends.
